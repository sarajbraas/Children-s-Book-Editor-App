# The Tale of Peter Rabbit -- Narration Analysis

A plain-English companion to the emotion/prosody tagging, for the author and for the person producing the voice-clone audio. No code or tags here -- just the reasoning behind the choices.

## What This Story Is About

Underneath its garden chase, this is a story about the cost of disobedience and the pull of appetite against good sense. Peter's mother gives a clear rule with a genuinely dark reason behind it, and Peter ignores it because the forbidden garden is simply more tempting than the safe lane. Everything that follows — the terror, the near-captures, the lost clothes, the sick stomach — flows directly from that one choice.

But Potter never wags a finger. The real emotional core is the affectionate, knowing gap between how thrilling Peter's adventure is and how quietly it costs him. The book lets a child feel the excitement of the chase while an adult reader smiles at the gentle justice of camomile tea in bed versus blackberries for supper. It's a story that loves its naughty hero even as it lets him get his comeuppance.

## The Voice of This Book

The narrator sits just outside the story, telling it with warmth and a dry, knowing wit rather than getting swept up in Peter's panic. Think of an adult reading aloud who finds Peter's misadventures both alarming and quietly amusing — measured and composed even when describing a frantic chase, occasionally leaning in to confide directly with the reader. The pace is unhurried and the humor is deadpan, never broad.

That said, the emotional range is real and reaches high at its peaks: genuine terror in the chase, real tears when Peter gives himself up for lost, and loud, sharp bursts of energy at the escape beats. The trick is that the narrator stays gently composed even at those peaks — the urgency lives in the pace and content, not in the narrator abandoning their poise. The ceiling is high and can get loud, but it always returns to that soothing, wry storytelling voice.

## The Humor in This Story

The comedy here runs on two quiet, sincere engines rather than any broad jokes: the narrator's deadpan understatement and the running contrast between the well-behaved siblings and their reckless brother.

### The narrator's deadpan understatement

Potter's funniest moments come from the narrator describing something with mild, mock-serious composure that's wildly out of proportion to the drama. Peter gorges himself and then, 'feeling rather sick,' goes looking for parsley — the flat matter-of-factness is the joke. Later the narrator notes he was 'very damp with sitting in that can,' treating a life-or-death hiding spot as a minor inconvenience. The humor works because the narrator refuses to get excited; the gap between the calm phrasing and the actual stakes is where the laugh lives.

> "Also he was very damp with sitting in that can."

Toss it off almost as an afterthought — dry, unbothered, a small knowing lilt, as if it barely merits mentioning.

### The good-bunnies-versus-Peter contrast

From the start the three obedient sisters dutifully gather blackberries while Peter bolts for the forbidden garden, and the book saves the payoff for the very last lines. Peter, sick in bed, gets a dose of camomile tea, while Flopsy, Mopsy and Cotton-tail sit down to a lovely supper. The comedy is the quiet symmetry of it — nobody scolds Peter, the outcome simply speaks for itself. It should feel like gentle justice, not a lesson.

> "Flopsy, Mopsy and Cottontail had bread and milk and blackberries for supper."

Deliver with gentle, knowing warmth and a touch of finality — let the contrast land on its own without any nudging or moralizing emphasis.

## Recurring Threads to Track Across the Story

### Peter's clothes

The brand-new blue jacket with brass buttons and the shoes are a genuine plot thread, not decoration. Introduce the jacket with a note of doomed pride — 'quite new' should carry a small knowing regret, because we sense it's about to be lost. The shoes go first among the cabbages and potatoes, the buttons literally trap him in the net and nearly cost his life, and the jacket is left behind in his escape. When the clothes reappear as McGregor's scarecrow and again in the closing tally, voice those beats with quiet, wry finality — this is the second outfit lost in a fortnight, and that's the whole gentle point.

### The narrator's confiding asides

Twice the narrator steps outside the action to speak directly and knowingly to the reader — 'I think he might have got away altogether...' and 'I am sorry to say that Peter was not very well...'. These want a distinct, gently wry, slightly-outside-the-story voice, warmer and more intimate than the in-scene chase narration, like the reader is being let in on a private observation. Keep them clearly separate in tone from Peter's urgency.

### The chase energy versus the composed narrator

Throughout the middle of the book, the content is frantic — Peter rushing, wriggling, jumping, sneezing at the worst moment — but the narrator stays measured and wry. The tension should live in quickened pace and breathless momentum rather than in the narrator shouting. Several action beats cut off mid-sentence (a trailing dash into the next line); keep the forward lean across those breaks so the urgency carries without the storytelling voice losing its poise.

## Where the Emotional Read Isn't What the Bare Words Suggest

A few places where reading the words alone, without this context, would lead to the wrong performance:

- The word-by-word 'He / Ate / Some / Radishes' isn't a mistake or a solemn moment — it's a deliberate comic build. Give it a bouncy, deliberate rhythm, each word landing like a step in Peter's greedy overeating, so it sets up the dry 'feeling rather sick' beat that follows.

- Peter's sneeze, 'Kertyschoo!', is the sound Peter himself makes — and crucially it's the sound that betrays his hiding place. The lines around it are hushed with hiding tension, so the sneeze should burst out suddenly and loud against that quiet, then the narrator settles back to wry composure. It's an involuntary disaster, not an excited exclamation.

- The line 'Peter gave himself up for lost and shed big tears' and the later 'Peter began to cry' are the story's genuine emotional low points, easy to skate past because the surrounding narration is so dry. Let these land tenderly and for real — the wry stance keeps them from tipping into melodrama, but this is actual despair and fear, not something played for a laugh.

- When the mother says her husband 'was put in a pie by Mrs. McGregor,' resist the urge to play it either as broad comedy or as grief. It's a solemn, matter-of-fact warning to children with a quiet undertone of sorrow — ominous but composed, which is exactly what makes McGregor's garden feel genuinely dangerous rather than silly.

- The 'lippity--lippity--' passage is Peter's tentative hopping, and the sound is doing the emotional work: he's shaken and cautiously recovering from fright. Deliver it soft and light with a gentle bouncing rhythm and small pauses on the dashes — not energetic, but wary and slow, a frightened little animal creeping back into motion.

- At the very end, 'I am sorry to say that Peter was not very well during the evening' is affectionate mock-sympathy, not real concern. The narrator isn't sorry at all — it's the dry setup for the camomile-tea-versus-blackberries punchline. The trailing 'But--' needs a small held beat so the final contrast can land.
